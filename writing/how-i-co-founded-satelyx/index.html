How I got into the space industry and co-founded SATELYX

I’m João Fernandes, co-founder and VP of Operations at SATELYX, a UK-based company that builds and operates its own satellites for shared in-orbit demonstration (IOD) missions. This is how I got there from a career in deep tech business development, the problem SATELYX solves, what my job involves and why Portugal is so well placed in space.

25 November 2026 · Ler em português

When I tell people I work in the space industry, they’re usually surprised, and often a little impressed, as if I’d made it into some club for geniuses that hardly anyone gets into. They’re even more surprised when I tell them that four or five years ago I was a business developer at a deep tech startup that had nothing to do with space.

Most people picture the space industry as NASA: rocket scientists, aerospace engineers, astronauts, people with PhDs, years and years of study and a 200 IQ. And the takeaway is usually the same: if that’s not you, space isn’t for you. I don’t blame anyone for thinking that. Two years ago I thought exactly the same. How was I supposed to work in space when I’d never studied anything to do with it?

I now know that couldn’t be further from the truth, and that picture needs bringing back down to Earth. Today’s space sector needs a lot more than scientists and engineers. Commercial space has turned space into a business too, built by companies and startups that need what every company needs: people who can sell, build partnerships, run operations, set strategy and grow the company. Scientists and engineers are as essential as ever, but they can’t do it on their own.

My own career is what showed me that. I’ve spent the last five years in deep tech and business development, starting startups and growing companies, and it taught me how much the industry needs people with startup experience. Space needs startups, and those startups need people like me. That’s how it all started.

How I joined SATELYX

For anyone who doesn’t know, I’m one of the co-founders of SATELYX, where I’m VP of Operations. I didn’t come to the team as an outsider, though: we’d already been working together on other projects. And the drive to build a space startup goes back a long way. One of my co-founders, Kenneth Tong, spent 15 years working on Singapore’s military satellite programmes and had always wanted to start a company in the space sector. He and the friends and colleagues he studied with had been talking for years about what commercial space was missing, because they had lived the problem first-hand.

The idea started taking shape in 2024, in Tokyo, which is funny, because I’m writing this in Tokyo in 2026. That’s when we came together around it. Back then it looked a bit different from what SATELYX is today, but it was already about satellites and getting them launched. In 2025 the team started building it properly, with the product taking shape, and by the end of 2025 and the start of 2026 the idea had sharpened into what we have now. Then in 2026 we ramped everything up: working with partners and developing and planning our first SATELYX-led mission.

Without them, there would be no SATELYX. But without the rest of us, me included, there wouldn’t be one either. Half the team comes from engineering, with experience building space missions and satellites. The other half is like me: venture builders and business developers who understand strategy, how the business works and what it takes to grow it. I think that mix is what makes SATELYX special, and it’s why we can move so quickly.

What I had to learn, and what I brought

The start was really, really hard. I had to get to grips with all the technical vocabulary and understand how the market actually works and what it takes: what a launch involves, what a satellite really is, what goes inside one. Most people have a rough idea of these things, but they’re far more complex in reality, and the sector has a lot more nuance than it looks. I never studied any of this, so I didn’t have the foundations. Working every day with people who do made a huge difference.

I wouldn’t point to one moment where my startup experience made the difference. It’s more what I still do today: taking a project from zero to 10, or in this case to 100. Getting a product off the ground and making people notice it and care means setting up a lot at once: operations, social media, how ideas and strategy get organised. Without startup experience, that’s very hard to pull off. Because I can work very quickly, I got all of that set up fast, so the rest of the team could focus on strategy instead of worrying about the more mundane stuff.

The problem SATELYX solves

So what’s the problem? If you’re outside the industry it isn’t obvious, but it’s a big one, and the easiest way to explain it is with a car.

Say a startup has built a new car part that makes cars more efficient. They can test almost everything in a lab, but every investor and customer is going to ask the same question: does it work in an actual car? The only way to answer that is to put it in a car and drive it on real roads and a real track. Until then, they have a good idea, not proof.

Space has exactly the same problem, just on a much bigger scale. A team building a sensor, part of a communications system or a propulsion system has to prove it works where it’s actually going to be used, which is in orbit. But building a satellite is nothing like building a car. It costs 100 times as much, and it comes with extra bureaucracy, mission operations, launch procurement and a rocket. It’s 100 or 1,000 times more complex, and most startups can’t take all of that on by themselves.

That’s where everything gets stuck. Until the technology is proven in space, investors won’t fund it and customers won’t buy it. But proving it in space takes money. It’s a classic chicken-and-egg problem, and it’s exactly what SATELYX was set up to solve.

What SATELYX does

SATELYX builds and operates its own satellites for shared in-orbit demonstration (IOD) missions, helping hardware and software developers prove their technology in space and accelerate their route to market. Several developers fly on the same satellite and share the cost of the mission. We integrate their technology, take responsibility for launch, operate the mission and return evidence of how it performed, so the developer can show customers and investors results from orbit.

Software teams can take part too. They bring a model, an algorithm or a data pipeline, we deploy it on the onboard compute of the satellite and return results from orbit, and they keep owning and running their application.

We call the product IOD as a Service, and our tagline sums up the idea: Prove once. Deploy everywhere.

Technologies validated on our missions join the SATELYX catalogue, together with what we learned integrating and operating them. Our objective is to use that catalogue to build and deliver turnkey satellites and missions for government agencies and enterprise customers, so technology that has already flown can be selected again. For the developer, that means further commercial opportunities beyond the first demonstration.

Where SATELYX is today

Mission 0 flew with a partner in Q4 2024 and covered technology transfer and edge-compute experiments in orbit. SATELYX was not the prime on that mission. Our first SATELYX-led mission, VLEO Pathfinder, is planned as a shared, multi-payload mission in very low Earth orbit, with a planning target of Q4 2027. SATELYX is based in the UK, with operations across the UK, Europe, Japan and the wider Asia-Pacific region.

What I do at SATELYX

My job is mainly operations and commercial execution. I spend a lot of time with customers, partners and everyone else around the business, turning those conversations into agreements and making sure what we agree actually gets done.

But it goes beyond that. My real goal is to help the team grow and to make sure a small team like ours can compete with much bigger ones. Sometimes that means taking work off other people’s plates so they can focus on what matters most. Sometimes it means building internal tools and workflows that let the team move much faster. It’s the same kind of work I’ve done at other companies, applied to space and to the commercial side of this business.

A big part of the job is being on the road. I’ve spent a lot of time in Taiwan and Japan, and in the UK too, because we’re building the business locally in each of these markets. People assume you can do this remotely, but in this business you can’t. You need to be in the room with people and meet the market face to face, especially in Asia, and in Japan in particular, where markets tend to be more closed and you won’t get far over video calls.

The other reason is the team. I travel to be with them and work side by side every day, so we can move faster. From Portugal there’s a time difference of eight or nine hours: when I’m waking up, my co-founders are going to bed, and when I’m going to bed, they’re waking up. Being in the same place is a strategic choice. It means we can work every single day, without letting up, to build the product and get it to market. Things work much better in person, and that’s what this stage needs. That’s why I’m here, giving everything I’ve got to make SATELYX win.

It’s a challenge I love. I never expected to end up in this field, and yet I can hold my own in conversations and find my way around it. People don’t look at me and think I have no idea what I’m talking about. I’ve learned a huge amount working in the industry over the last few years, and especially this past year, and the travel is a big part of that. Every day I’m working with a very experienced team that pushes me to grow, and with partners on the ground who teach me a lot, and it has sharpened the way I work. In the end, everything I do here is something I’ve done before at other companies: understand the product, explain it to the market and prove that it works and that people need it.

How to get into the space industry without being an engineer

As I said at the start, you don’t need to be an engineer or an astronaut to work in space today. It depends on how you want to get in.

One way is to build a downstream startup, which the industry badly needs right now. Downstream means taking satellite data and turning it into decisions or a product people need, for example to fight wildfires or track supply chains. A lot of downstream applications are being built today, and you don’t need space experience for them. What you do need is to understand what data you can get from space and how to turn it into a product. That side is mostly software.

The other way is to join a space company that’s already up and running. My advice there is to focus on what you already know. You don’t need to retrain or go back to school. Whatever field you’re trained in, whether that’s finance, accounting, operations like me, business development or sales, you can apply it at a space company. All of it is needed in the space sector right now.

Portugal and space

I’m very keen to bring SATELYX closer to Portugal, because Portugal is one of the countries best placed to build a big space sector. With the Azores and Madeira, its exclusive economic zone covers about 1.7 million km², one of the largest in the world, and the country is investing seriously in space.

Santa Maria, in the Azores, already has a licensed private spaceport, and the government is planning a second, public launch base on the same island, run by the Air Force. A satellite factory with clean rooms is due to open in Alverca on 11 December. Portugal now has two ESA business incubation centres, ESA BIC Centro in Coimbra and ESA BIC Tagus in Oeiras, to bring in more companies building space technology. And at the last ESA ministerial meeting, in 2025, Portugal committed €204.8 million, its largest contribution ever and 51% more in real terms than in 2022.

But Portugal has the same problem as everyone else: many companies can’t get the flight heritage they need, the proof that their technology has already worked in space. That’s what SATELYX can offer. We can also go further and help connect Portugal with the UK, Japan and Taiwan, sharing knowledge and manufacturing capacity. Taiwan, for one, has a lot of manufacturing capability that could be put to use in Portugal.

Right now I’m putting a lot of work into the Portugal side, talking to a lot of people there to understand the ecosystem better and work out how we can help.
